Lesson 03 · The core moves

Pattern recognition

Once a problem is in pieces, the next question is which pieces are secretly the same piece. Spotting that is how you stop solving the same problem twice.

Time ~10 min Win You can turn several similar parts into one reusable rule Mission link Move 2 of six — it is what makes a solution small

1Two kinds of sameness

After Lesson 2 you have a list of sub-problems. Read down that list and you will usually find that some of them are the same shape. There are two ways that happens, and they need slightly different responses.

Repetition is the easy one: the same step happens again and again. Similarity is subtler: the steps are not identical, but they differ only in one detail — a name, a number, a place — and that detail could be handed in as a value.

Key idea A pattern is a promise that you only have to solve this once. If two parts differ only in a value, they are one part with a hole in it.

2From three cases to one rule

Here is a real list of sub-problems from a morning routine: 

make coffee
make toast
make porridge

Three tasks, three recipes — or one pattern. Look at what they share: each one is "put something in a container, apply heat, wait, take it out". The only thing that changes is what and how long. 

prepare(food, seconds):
    put food in container
    apply heat
    wait seconds
    take food out

You have replaced three specific cases with one general rule. That is called generalisation, and it is the payoff of pattern recognition: the rule now covers breakfasts you have not thought of yet.

3Patterns are a claim — test them

A pattern is a guess about the world, and guesses can be wrong. The way to test one is to hunt for a counter-example: a case that looks like it fits but does not.

Does "apply heat" cover orange juice? No — and that is useful. It tells you the pattern is about cooked breakfasts, not all breakfasts. You have not broken the pattern; you have found its boundary, which is exactly what you needed to know.

Common mistake Forcing a pattern because it is tidy. If you have to add three special cases to make the rule fit, the rule is wrong. A pattern that needs constant exceptions is not a pattern — it is a coincidence.

4Why this matters so much

Every time you find a real pattern, three things happen at once. Your solution gets shorter, because one rule replaces many cases. It gets easier to fix, because a bug in the rule is fixed in one place. And it gets easier to extend, because a new case that fits the pattern needs no new work at all.

This is the move that separates a solution that works from a solution that keeps working. It is also the move that leads directly into Lesson 4: once you have a general rule, you need somewhere to put it.

✓ Your win for today

You took three separate tasks and collapsed them into one rule with a hole in it, then tested the rule by looking for a case it does not cover. That is pattern recognition done properly.

5Do this next

  1. List five things you did today and find the one rule that covers three of them.
  2. Write the rule with its "hole" — the value that changes between cases.
  3. Find one counter-example that shows where your rule stops working.

Lesson 02 · The core moves

Decomposition

Big problems are not solved by trying harder. They are solved by being cut into pieces small enough that each piece is obvious on its own.

Time ~10 min Win You can split a real problem into non-overlapping parts Mission link Move 1 of six — everything else depends on it

1Why one big problem is the wrong size

"Plan a trip to another country" is not a task. It is a fog. You cannot start it, you cannot finish it, and you cannot tell whether you are halfway. The problem is not that it is hard — it is that it is the wrong size.

Decomposition is the act of cutting it down to size. Instead of one fog, you get a handful of tasks you could each finish in an afternoon: choose dates, book flights, find somewhere to stay, sort out a visa, pack.

Key idea A sub-problem is the right size when you can solve it without thinking about the rest of the problem. If you keep having to remember the whole trip, the cut is in the wrong place.

2A good split covers everything and overlaps nothing

Not every cut is a good cut. A useful decomposition has two properties:

  • It covers the whole problem. Every part of the job belongs to some sub-problem. Nothing is left over.
  • The parts do not overlap. No piece of work is done twice, and no two sub-problems fight over the same decision.

A split with both properties is called a partition. It is the cleanest kind of decomposition, and it is worth aiming for even when you cannot quite reach it.

3Some parts must wait for others

Once you have your parts, they are not all equal. Some can be done in any order. Others cannot start until another one is finished — you cannot book flights before you have chosen dates.

That relationship is a dependency, and finding the dependencies is what turns a pile of sub-problems into a plan. Draw an arrow from each sub-problem to the ones that must wait for it, and the order of work falls out. 

choose dates  ──▶  book flights
              ──▶  find somewhere to stay
              ──▶  sort out a visa

pack          (no dependency — can happen any time)

Notice that "pack" has no arrows into it. It is independent, which means it can be done first, last, or in parallel. Independent parts are a gift: they are the ones you can hand to someone else, or do while you wait.

Common mistake Splitting by time instead of by work. "First I do the morning, then the afternoon" is not decomposition — it is a schedule. A real split produces parts that could each be solved on their own, in any order that respects the dependencies.

4How small is too small?

There is no single right answer, but there is a wrong one at both ends. Split too coarsely and your "sub-problems" are still fogs. Split too finely and you spend all your time managing the pieces instead of solving them.

The practical test: can you describe the sub-problem in one sentence, and could you hand it to someone else without further explanation? If yes, it is the right size. If you need a paragraph, split again. If it is a single trivial action, you have gone too far.

✓ Your win for today

You took a fog-sized problem and cut it into parts that cover everything, overlap nothing, and have a clear order of work. That is a real decomposition, and it is the foundation the next three lessons build on.

5Do this next

  1. Decompose "get ready for work in the morning" into 5–7 parts, then draw the dependencies.
  2. Find one part of that routine that is independent of all the others.
  3. Take a problem you are avoiding and split it until each part fits in one sentence.

Lesson 07 · Judging the work

Generalisation

You solved one problem. Generalisation is the move that turns that solution into one that solves a whole family of problems — without solving any of them again.

Time ~10 min Win You can widen a solution to cover a class of problems, and know when not to Mission link Move 6 — the move that makes the work pay off twice

1From one case to a family

In Lesson 3 you found patterns inside a problem — three similar steps collapsed into one rule. Generalisation is the same idea pointed outward: you have a solution that works for one case, and you ask what family of cases it could work for.

You sorted a list of 40 names. Could the same steps sort 40 numbers? 400 names? A list of anything that can be put in order? If yes, you have not solved one problem — you have solved a class of them, and every future member of that class is now free.

Key idea A specific solution solves today's problem. A general solution solves today's problem and every problem like it that you have not met yet. That is where the leverage is.

2Find the part that varies

To generalise, you look for the one thing that changes between cases and lift it out into a value you supply. Everything else stays fixed — that fixed part is the general solution.

This is exactly the move you made in Lesson 3 when three recipes became prepare(food, seconds). The food and the time were the parts that varied; the preparing was the part that stayed. Generalisation is that move applied to a whole solution rather than a single step.

Common mistake Generalising too early. If you have seen one case, you do not know what varies. Wait until you have two or three real cases before you lift anything out — otherwise you will invent a parameter that no future case needs.

3Know where the family ends

A general solution is a claim: this works for every case in the family. Like any claim, it can be wrong. Your job is to find the boundary — the point where the family stops and a different solution is needed. 

sort(names)          works for: any list of things
                                  that can be compared

sort(names)          breaks on:  a list of mixed types
                                  (a number and a word
                                   cannot be compared)

The boundary is not a failure.
It is the edge of the family.

Finding the boundary is the same skill as the counter-example from Lesson 3, scaled up. You are hunting for the case that looks like it belongs to the family but does not — and when you find it, you have learned exactly how far your solution reaches.

4Generalise the thinking, not just the code

The most valuable generalisation is not a solution you can reuse — it is a method you can reuse. Once you have solved a few problems with the six moves, you notice that the moves themselves are the general solution.

Decompose, find the pattern, abstract, write the steps, evaluate, generalise. That sequence is not specific to sorting names or planning trips. It is the general procedure for turning any messy problem into something a computer can run — and it is the thing this course was actually teaching you.

Key idea You started this course learning six moves. You finish it having generalised them into one method that works on problems nobody has shown you yet.

✓ Your win for today

You widened a solution from one case to a whole family, identified the part that varies and lifted it out, found the boundary where the family ends, and recognised the six moves themselves as a reusable method.

Lesson 01 · The loop

What computational thinking is

Programming is not typing. It is deciding, precisely, what has to happen — and that decision is made before a single line of code exists. This lesson shows you the six moves that decision is made of.

Time ~10 min Win You can name the six moves and say what each one is for Mission link This is the map for the whole course

1A computer is a very obedient idiot

A computer will do exactly what you tell it. That sounds like a strength, and it is — but it is also the whole difficulty. It has no common sense to fill in the gaps you leave. If you tell a person "make me a sandwich", they will manage. If you tell a computer "make me a sandwich", it will sit there, because it does not know what a sandwich is, where the bread is, or that you meant the kitchen.

So the work of programming is not persuading the machine. It is removing every gap from your own description of the problem, until nothing is left to guess.

Key idea A computer cannot resolve ambiguity. Every gap you leave becomes a bug. Your job is to close the gaps before you write anything.

2Six moves, one loop

Closing those gaps is not one skill — it is six, and they feed each other. Together they are called computational thinking.

Notice the arrows go round. You do not do these once, in a straight line. You decompose, spot a pattern, abstract it, write the steps — then you judge the result and widen it to a family of problems. And when you test the steps, you usually discover your decomposition was wrong and you go round again. That is not failure. That is the method.

3Input, output, and the sentence in between

Before any of the six moves, you need to know what you are aiming at. The trick is to write one sentence that says what must be true when you are finished. Everything else is judged against that sentence.

A useful problem statement has three parts: what you are given (the input), what you must produce (the output), and the rule that connects them. 

Given:  a list of 40 names, unsorted
Produce: the same names, in alphabetical order
Rule:   every name appears exactly once, and no name comes
        before a name that should precede it

That is already a real piece of computational thinking. You have not written a single step yet, but you have made the problem checkable: when you are done, you can look at the output and know whether it is right.

Common mistake "Sort the names" is not a problem statement — it is a wish. It does not say what "sorted" means, whether duplicates are allowed, or whether the input is already partly sorted. A wish cannot be tested. A statement can.

4Why this comes before code

You could learn Python syntax in a weekend. People do. What takes longer is being able to look at a messy situation and see the structure inside it — and that skill transfers to every language, and to problems that have nothing to do with computers at all.

The next four lessons take one core move each. Lessons 6 and 7 add the two moves that come after you have written something — judging it, then widening it. Lesson 8 puts all six back together on a problem you will meet in Lesson 1 and solve properly at the end.

✓ Your win for today

You can name the six moves — decompose, find patterns, abstract, write an algorithm, evaluate, generalise — and you can write a problem statement with an input, an output and a rule that makes the answer checkable.

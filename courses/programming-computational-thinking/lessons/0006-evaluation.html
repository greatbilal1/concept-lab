Lesson 06 · Judging the work

Evaluation

A solution that works is not the same as a solution that is good. Evaluation is how you tell the difference — and how you decide whether to keep the one you have or go round the loop again.

Time ~10 min Win You can judge a solution against criteria, not just against "it works" Mission link Move 5 — the check that closes the loop

1"It works" is the lowest bar

In Lesson 5 you learned to trace an algorithm and confirm it produces the right output. That is correctness, and it matters — but it is only the first question. A solution can be correct and still be terrible.

Consider two algorithms that both sort a list of names correctly. One takes a second. The other takes an hour. Both are correct. Only one is good. Correctness tells you the answer is right; evaluation tells you whether the way you got there is worth keeping.

Key idea Correctness is a yes/no question. Quality is a comparison. Evaluation is the act of comparing your solution against something — another solution, a requirement, or the version you had yesterday.

2Four things worth measuring

You cannot evaluate against nothing. You need criteria — the specific qualities you are going to judge. For most solutions, four criteria cover almost everything.

Notice that these criteria pull against each other. Making a solution faster often makes it harder to read. Making it simpler often makes it slower. There is rarely a solution that wins on all four — evaluation is choosing which trade-off is right for this problem.

Common mistake Evaluating against a criterion you never wrote down. "This feels cleaner" is not an evaluation — it is a preference. Name the criterion first, then judge.

3Compare, don't just inspect

The strongest form of evaluation is comparison. Instead of asking "is this good?", ask "is this better than the alternative?" That question has an answer you can defend. 

Solution A — check every name against every other name
  correct: yes
  steps for 40 names: 780 comparisons

Solution B — split the list, sort each half, merge them
  correct: yes
  steps for 40 names: 220 comparisons

Same result. B does a third of the work.
Evaluation: keep B.

You do not need to run anything to do this. Counting the steps by hand — the same skill as tracing — is enough to compare two solutions and pick the better one.

4When to stop and when to go round again

Evaluation has one job: decide whether to keep the solution or refine it. That decision is the hinge of the whole loop.

Keep it when it meets the criteria well enough for the problem at hand. Go round again when a criterion is badly missed, or when you have learned something that makes a better solution possible. And be honest about the third option: sometimes the right answer is that the problem was not worth solving this carefully, and "good enough" really is good enough.

Key idea Refining forever is not rigour, it is a way of never finishing. Evaluation exists so you can stop with a reason.

✓ Your win for today

You judged a solution against named criteria instead of a feeling, compared two correct solutions by counting their work, and decided — with a reason — whether to keep the solution or go round the loop again.

5Do this next

  1. Take the breakfast algorithm you wrote in Lesson 5.
  2. Write down two criteria you would judge it by — say, speed and clarity.
  3. Write a second version that is better on one criterion and worse on the other.
  4. Decide which one you would keep, and say why in one sentence.
Primary source George Pólya, How to Solve It (1945) — the "looking back" stage: after solving a problem, examine the solution and ask whether it can be checked, improved, or used for something else.

Lesson 03 · Logic

Combining conditions

Real decisions are rarely one question. "Is the user logged in and an admin?" "Is the file missing or empty?" This lesson is about the three operators that glue booleans together — and the two laws that let you rewrite them without changing what they mean.

Time ~10 min Win Build a truth table from scratch Mission link How one condition becomes many

1Three operators

Python has exactly three logical operators, and they are spelled as words rather than symbols: and, or and not. That is deliberate — and reads better than &&, and it means you never confuse a logical operator with a bitwise one.

and and or take two values. not takes one. All three return a boolean when given booleans.

2The truth table is the whole story

Two inputs means four possible combinations, so a complete description of and and or fits in four rows. This table is worth memorising, because every logical bug you will ever write is a row you got wrong.

Key idea and is true only when both sides are true. or is false only when both sides are false. not flips one value.

3De Morgan's laws

There are two identities that let you push a not inward through a logical expression. They are named after Augustus De Morgan, and they are the reason you can always rewrite a negated condition into a positive one: 

not (a and b)   ==   (not a) or  (not b)
not (a or  b)   ==   (not a) and (not b)

In words: to negate an and, negate both sides and switch to or. To negate an or, negate both sides and switch to and. The operator always flips.

Why this matters Negated conditions are hard to read. not (logged_in and is_admin) is fine, but not logged_in or not is_admin is often clearer in context — and De Morgan's laws guarantee the two are identical, so you can pick whichever reads better without changing behaviour.

4Build a truth table yourself

Here is the win for today. Take the expression a and (b or not a) and work out its value for all four combinations of a and b. Do it by hand before you look at the answer. 

a      b      not a   b or not a   a and (b or not a)
False  False  True    True         False
False  True   True    True         False
True   False  False   False        False
True   True   False   True         True

The whole expression turns out to be the same as a and b. That is not obvious from looking at it — which is exactly why truth tables exist. When a condition confuses you, write out its rows.

✓ Your win for today

You can now combine any number of conditions into one, and you can prove what the result means by writing its truth table. You also know De Morgan's laws, which let you rewrite a negated condition into a readable one.

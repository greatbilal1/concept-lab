Lesson 04 · Notation

Dropping the constants

Lesson 03 gave you the notation. This lesson explains the rule that makes it useful: when you write $O(\ldots)$, you throw away the constant factors and keep only the growth shape. Here is why that is not cheating.

Time ~10 min Win Simplify any cost expression to its Big O Mission link The rule that makes two solutions comparable

1Two loops, one shape

Suppose you count the work in two programs that both scan a list of $n$ items. Program A does one comparison per item. Program B does three — a comparison, a bounds check, and a counter bump. Their exact counts are $n$ and $3n$.

Which is "better"? On paper, A. In Big O, they are the same: both are $O(n)$. That feels like throwing away information, so let's see what is actually being thrown away.

The $3$ in $3n$ is a constant factor. It depends on the language, the compiler, and how you wrote the loop — not on the algorithm. Change the language and the $3$ might become a $2$ or a $7$. But the $n$ stays. The $n$ is the part that belongs to the algorithm.

2The rule, stated plainly

To reduce any cost expression to Big O, apply two moves:

  • Drop constant factors. $3n$ becomes $n$. $\tfrac{1}{2}n^2$ becomes $n^2$.
  • Keep only the fastest-growing term. $n^2 + n + 5$ becomes $n^2$.

The second move is the one people find strange. Why does the $n$ vanish when there is an $n^2$ next to it? Because for large $n$, the $n^2$ term dominates so completely that the rest is noise.

At $n = 1{,}000$, $n^2$ is $1{,}000{,}000$ while $n + 5$ is $1{,}005$ — about a tenth of one percent. At $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ the smaller terms are invisible. Big O is a statement about the limit, so it keeps only what survives.

Key idea Big O keeps the shape and drops the scale. Constants and smaller terms change how fast the curve climbs, but never how it is shaped.

3Simplify a real expression

Here is a cost expression you might get from counting a nested loop with a little setup work: $3n^2 + 10n + 42$. Step through the simplification below and watch each piece get discarded for a reason.

The answer is $O(n^2)$. Notice that the $10n$ term was not "small" — for $n = 10$ it is $100$, larger than the $42$. It was dropped because it grows more slowly than $n^2$, and Big O only cares about the long run.

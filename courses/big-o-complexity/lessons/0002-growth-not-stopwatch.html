Lesson 02 · Growth

Growth, not stopwatch

The obvious way to compare two programs is to run them and see which finishes first. This lesson shows why that answer is almost useless — and what to measure instead.

Time ~10 min Win Predict which program wins at scale Mission link Why Big O is the honest comparison

1The stopwatch lies to you

Suppose you write two programs that sort a list. You run both on a list of 10 items. Program X finishes in 2 milliseconds, Program Y in 5. You conclude X is better and move on.

Now the list has 10,000 items. Program Y finishes in 40 milliseconds. Program X is still running after two minutes. What happened?

Nothing changed about the programs. What changed is the input size, and the two programs respond to it completely differently. A stopwatch on a small input measures the wrong thing.

2Three things a stopwatch cannot separate

When you time a program, the number you get is a blend of at least three different things:

  • The algorithm's growth — the thing you actually want to know.
  • The constant factor — how many machine instructions each step costs.
  • The machine and the moment — your CPU, your RAM, what else is running.

Big O deliberately throws away the second and third so you can see the first. That is not sloppiness — it is the point. The constant factor is a detail of your implementation; the growth is a property of the algorithm, and it is the part that decides what happens at scale.

Key idea A stopwatch measures one run on one machine. Big O measures the shape of the cost curve — and the shape is what survives when the input grows.

3Where the curves cross

Here is the surprising part. A slower-growing algorithm can lose on small inputs and still win overall. If Program X does $100n$ operations and Program Y does $n^2$, then: 

n = 10      X: 1,000 ops      Y: 100 ops      Y wins
n = 100     X: 10,000 ops     Y: 10,000 ops   tie
n = 1,000   X: 100,000 ops    Y: 1,000,000    X wins
n = 10,000  X: 1,000,000      Y: 100,000,000  X wins by 100×

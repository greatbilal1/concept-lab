Lesson 07 · Space & trade-offs

Best, worst, average

A single algorithm can have three different costs depending on the input it gets. This lesson shows why "the complexity" is really three numbers, and which one you should quote when someone asks.

Time ~12 min Win Name the best, worst and average case Mission link The honest answer to "how fast is it?"

1One algorithm, three answers

Take linear search: walk a list from the front until you find the target. How much work does it do? It depends entirely on where the target is.

  • Target is the first item — one comparison. That is the best case.
  • Target is the last item, or missing — $n$ comparisons. That is the worst case.
  • Target is somewhere in the middle on average — about $n/2$ comparisons. That is the average case.

Same algorithm, three different costs. So when someone asks "what is the complexity of linear search?", the honest answer is a set of three, not one number.

2Which one do you quote?

By convention, when people say "the complexity" without qualification, they mean the worst case. There is a good reason: the worst case is the only one you can promise. If you tell a user "it is usually fast", you have promised nothing about the day it is slow.

The average case is the most useful in practice, but it is also the hardest to state honestly, because it depends on an assumption about what inputs you actually get. The best case is almost never worth quoting — it is the case you cannot rely on.

Key idea Best, worst and average are three different questions. Unqualified "Big O" means the worst case, because that is the only bound you can guarantee.

3Watch the three cases

Step through a linear search for a target that is not in the list. Watch the comparison count climb to $n$ — the worst case — and notice how far it is from the best case of one.

For a list of 8 items, the best case is 1 comparison, the average is about 4, and the worst is 8. All three are $O(n)$ in the sense that they grow with $n$ — but the constant in front is wildly different, and only the worst case is a promise.

4Recall the three cases

Fill these in from memory before the quiz.

✓ Your win for today

You can state the best, worst and average case of an algorithm separately, and you know that an unqualified Big O claim means the worst case. You will never be caught out by "but sometimes it is fast".

5Do this next

  1. Write down the best, worst and average case of finding a value in a sorted list with binary search. Which of the three is $O(1)$?
  2. Explain in one sentence why quoting the best case is usually misleading.
  3. Think of an algorithm whose worst case is rare but catastrophic. Why would you still design around the worst case? This sets up Lesson 08.

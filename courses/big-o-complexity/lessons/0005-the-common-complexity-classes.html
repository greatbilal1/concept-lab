Lesson 05 · Complexity classes

The common complexity classes

There are only a handful of growth shapes you will meet in practice, and they have names. Learn to recognise the six of them on sight and you can classify almost any algorithm you are handed.

Time ~12 min Win Name the class of any loop you read Mission link The vocabulary for comparing two solutions

1Six shapes, one ladder

You have already met $O(1)$, $O(n)$ and $O(\log n)$ in passing. In practice almost every algorithm lands on one of six rungs, and they are worth knowing in order from cheapest to most expensive:

  • $O(1)$ — constant. The work does not depend on $n$ at all.
  • $O(\log n)$ — logarithmic. Each step throws away a fraction of the input.
  • $O(n)$ — linear. One pass over the input.
  • $O(n \log n)$ — linearithmic. A linear pass that does a log-sized job per item.
  • $O(n^2)$ — quadratic. Every item paired with every other item.
  • $O(2^n)$ — exponential. Every item doubles the work.

The ladder matters because the gaps between rungs are enormous. Moving one rung down is usually worth more than any amount of micro-optimisation.

2What each shape looks like in code

The class is not a property of the language — it is a property of the loop structure. Read the shape of the loops and you can name the class without running anything.

Notice the pattern: one loop over $n$ is linear; a loop that halves is logarithmic; two nested loops over $n$ are quadratic; a loop that splits the input and recurses on both halves is exponential. The structure tells you the class.

Key idea Recognise the class from the loop structure, not from timing. Nested loops multiply; halving divides; recursion on both halves explodes.

3Watch the ladder at scale

Here is why the ladder is the whole game. Step through the table below and watch what happens to each class as $n$ grows from 10 to 1,000. The cheap rungs barely move; the expensive ones fall off a cliff.

At $n = 1{,}000$, $O(n \log n)$ is about $10{,}000$ operations and $O(n^2)$ is $1{,}000{,}000$ — a hundredfold difference from one rung. That is why "which class is this?" is the first question to ask about any algorithm.

You can look at a piece of code, read its loop structure, and name its complexity class — and you know the six rungs well enough to place a new algorithm on the ladder.

5Do this next

  1. Classify these by their loop structure: a single pass; two nested passes; a pass that halves each time; a pass that calls itself twice on half the input.
  2. Look up the complexity of the sort your language ships with. Which rung is it on, and why can it not be lower?

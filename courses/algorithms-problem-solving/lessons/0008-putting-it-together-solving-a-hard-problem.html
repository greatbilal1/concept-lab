Lesson 08 · Putting it together

Putting it together: solving a hard problem

You now have search, sort, and recursion in your toolkit. Real problems rarely need just one of them — they need you to combine them and choose wisely. Today you take a problem that looks hard, break it into pieces you already know, and reason about which combination is worth the cost.

Time ~14 min Win Break a hard problem into known pieces and justify your trade-off Mission link This is the whole course in one problem: decompose, choose, and reason about cost

1The problem

Here is the challenge. You have a list of a million order numbers, and you need to answer thousands of questions of the form "is order 482913 in the list?" A single linear search would scan up to a million items per question. Thousands of those questions would take far too long.

Nothing in this problem is new. It is the same sorted precondition from binary search, the same sorting from lessons four and five, and the same divide and conquer thinking from merge sort. The skill is recognising that and assembling the pieces.

Key idea Hard problems are usually familiar pieces in an unfamiliar arrangement. Your job is to decompose the problem, match each piece to a tool you know, and reason about the cost of the combination.

2Complexity and big-O vocabulary

To choose between approaches you need a shared language for cost. Complexity describes how the work grows as the input grows, and big-O notation is how we write it. Linear search is O(n): double the list and you roughly double the work. Binary search is O(log n): double the list and you add just one more step.

Sorting is O(n log n) with merge sort. That sounds worse than O(n), and for a single search it is. But you pay it once and then every one of your thousands of searches becomes O(log n) instead of O(n). That is the trade-off.

3The trade-off: precomputation

The decision comes down to how many questions you will ask. If you search once, sorting first is wasted effort. If you search thousands of times, sorting first is the best decision in the whole course — you spend n log n once to save n on every question that follows.

This is precomputation: doing work up front so that later work is cheap. It is the same idea as lesson four's "sorting is preparation", now with numbers attached so you can decide when it is worth it.

4Choosing your tools

When you meet a new problem, run through the toolkit. Is the data sorted, or can I sort it cheaply? Am I asking one question or many? Does the problem split into smaller copies of itself? Each answer points at a tool, and the cost vocabulary tells you whether the combination is worth it.

You will not always pick perfectly, and that is fine. The habit that matters is naming the trade-off out loud before you write code, instead of discovering the cost after it runs too slowly.

✓ Your win for today You can take a hard problem, break it into pieces you already know, and justify your choice with a complexity trade-off.

  2. Estimate the total cost of sorting once then running a thousand binary searches.
  3. Pick a problem from your own work and name which of today's tools it needs.

Lesson 05 · Sorting

Divide and conquer: merge sort

Selection sort and insertion sort both compare items one pair at a time, so they slow down sharply as the list grows. Merge sort takes a completely different route: split the list in half, sort each half the same way, then stitch the two sorted halves together. That one idea — divide and conquer — is the first algorithm in this course that scales.

Time ~12 min Win Trace a merge of two sorted lists and explain why splitting beats comparing every pair Mission link Divide and conquer is the pattern behind the fastest sorts and searches

1The divide and conquer idea

Merge sort rests on a simple observation: sorting two short lists is much easier than sorting one long list, and combining two already sorted lists is almost free. So it does exactly that, over and over.

The recipe has three steps. Divide the list into two halves. Conquer each half by sorting it the same way. Combine the two sorted halves into one sorted list. The recursion stops when a piece has zero or one item, because a single item is already sorted.

Key idea Divide and conquer: split the problem into smaller copies of itself, solve each copy, then combine the results. The combining step is where the real work happens.

2The merge step

Merging is the heart of the algorithm. You have two sorted lists and you want one sorted list. Look at the front item of each. Whichever is smaller is guaranteed to be the smallest item left overall, so you take it and move on. Repeat until one list runs out, then append whatever remains.

Each item is looked at once, so merging two lists of total size n costs about n steps. That is the key to the whole algorithm: the expensive part is linear, not quadratic.

Watch two sorted lists, [3, 7] and [2, 9], become one. At each step we compare the two front items and take the smaller one.

4Why it scales

Halving the list means the recursion is only about log n levels deep. At each level, the total amount of merging across all pieces is n. Multiply the levels by the work per level and you get roughly n log n — dramatically better than the n² of the simple sorts once the list gets large.

The price is memory. Merge sort needs a second array to hold the merged output, so unlike selection and insertion sort it is not in-place. It is, however, stable if you break ties by taking from the left list first.

✓ Your win for today You can trace a merge of two sorted lists step by step, and you can explain why divide and conquer turns an n² problem into an n log n one.

5Do this next

  1. Merge [1, 4, 6] and [2, 3, 8] by hand, writing the output after each comparison.
  2. Split [8, 3, 5, 1] all the way down to single items, then merge back up.
  3. Explain in one sentence why merging two sorted lists is cheaper than sorting one unsorted list.

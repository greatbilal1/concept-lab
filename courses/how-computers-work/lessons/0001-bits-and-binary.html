Lesson 01 · Numbers

Bits and binary

Everything a computer does — every photo, every song, every line of code — is ultimately a row of switches that are either on or off. This lesson is about that row, and about the surprising amount of meaning you can squeeze out of it.

Time ~10 min Win Count to 255 on your fingers Mission link The layer every other lesson sits on

1Why not just use ten?

You count in base 10 because you have ten fingers. A computer has no fingers — it has wires, and a wire is either carrying a current or it is not. Two states. That is not a limitation someone chose; it is what the physics gives you.

So the machine counts in base 2. Each position holds one of two symbols, 0 or 1. One such position is called a bit — short for binary digit.

Key idea A bit is not "a small number". A bit is one yes/no answer. Everything else in this course is built by stacking those answers.

2Place value is the whole trick

You already understand place value — you just have not thought about it. In 347, the 3 is worth three hundreds, not three. Each position is worth ten times the one to its right.

Binary works identically, except each position is worth twice the one to its right:

So 1011 in binary means:

  1     0     1     1
  ↓     ↓     ↓     ↓
  8  +  0  +  2  +  1   =  11

Read it right to left: the rightmost bit is worth 1, then 2, then 4, then 8. Add up the positions that hold a 1. That is the whole algorithm.

3Eight bits, 256 values

One bit gives you two values. Two bits give you four. Three give you eight. Each extra bit doubles the number of distinct patterns you can make.

Eight bits — one byte — gives you $2^8 = 256$ patterns, which is why a byte can hold any number from 0 to 255. This is not an arbitrary choice: it is the smallest power of two that comfortably holds a character of English text, and it became the standard unit of memory.

Why this matters later Every memory address you will ever see is a number of bytes. Every colour on your screen is three bytes — one each for red, green and blue. Every file size is a count of bytes. The byte is the atom of this whole subject.

4Count on your fingers

Here is the win for today. Hold up one hand. Your five fingers are five bits. Curl a finger down for 0, extend it for 1. Now count from zero, incrementing in binary: 

00000  =  0     (all curled)
00001  =  1     (thumb up)
00010  =  2     (index up, thumb down)
00011  =  3     (thumb + index)
00100  =  4     (middle up)
...
11111  =  31    (all five up)

Five fingers, thirty-one values. Both hands gives you ten bits and 1,023 values. You have been carrying a binary counter around your whole life.

✓ Your win for today

You can now convert any binary number to decimal by hand, and you know why the machine uses two symbols instead of ten. That is the foundation: every later lesson — hex, gates, memory addresses, floating point — is written in the language you just learned to read.

6Do this next

  1. Convert these by hand, then check with a calculator: 1010, 1111, 100000, 1100100.
  2. Go the other way. Write 42, 100 and 200 in binary. Hint: find the largest power of two that fits, subtract, repeat.
  3. Count to 31 on one hand, out loud, in binary. It feels absurd for about ninety seconds and then it becomes automatic.
Primary source Read _Code_ by Charles Petzold, Chapter 8 ("Relays and Gates") and Chapter 9 ("Our Ten Digits"). Petzold builds binary from telegraph relays, which is the most intuitive path into this material that exists.

Lesson 12 · Putting it together

Why programs are slow

Everything in this course now comes together. With bits, gates, memory, the CPU and the memory hierarchy in hand, you can look at a slow program and say why it is slow — before you change a single line.

Time ~12 min Win Classify three slow programs Mission link The payoff for the whole course

1The bottleneck

A program is a chain of work, and a chain is only as fast as its slowest link. The bottleneck is the part that everything else waits on. Optimising anything else is wasted effort.

Key idea Find the bottleneck first, then fix it. Guessing at performance is how you spend a week making code 5% faster while the real problem sits untouched.

2Three kinds of slow

Almost every performance problem is one of three shapes, and each has a different fix:

The classification matters because the fixes are completely different. Making a CPU-bound loop faster means better algorithms or fewer operations. Making a memory-bound loop faster means better locality. Making an I/O-bound program faster means overlapping the waiting with other work.

3Amdahl's law, in one sentence

Suppose 90% of your program's time is in one function. You make that function infinitely fast. How much faster is the program? 

before:  90% in the hot part  +  10% elsewhere  =  100%
after:    0% in the hot part  +  10% elsewhere  =   10%

speedup = 100 / 10 = 10×   — and no more, ever

That is Amdahl's law: the part you did not optimise sets a hard ceiling on your speedup. It is why profiling comes before optimising — you need to know which part is the 90%.

The mindset Measure, do not guess. A profiler tells you where the time actually goes, and it is almost never where you assumed. Every experienced engineer has a story about optimising the wrong function for a day.

4Classify three programs

Here is the win — and the final exercise of the course. For each program, name the bottleneck: 

A. A loop doing 10 million floating-point multiplications
B. A loop summing a 1 GB array in random order
C. A script that downloads 500 files one after another

A is CPU-bound — the arithmetic is the work. B is memory-bound — random access defeats the cache, so the CPU waits on RAM. C is I/O-bound — the network is the limit, and the CPU is idle almost the whole time.

Three programs, three bottlenecks, three different fixes. You just did the thing this whole course was building toward.

✓ Your win for today

You can now identify a bottleneck, classify it as CPU-, memory- or I/O-bound, and explain why Amdahl's law caps your speedup. More than that: you can now reason about the machine underneath your code. That was the mission — and you have finished it.

6Do this next

  1. Take a slow script you have written and classify its bottleneck. Is it CPU-, memory- or I/O-bound? What is the fix?
  2. Apply Amdahl's law. If 50% of a program is optimisable, what is the maximum possible speedup? What if it is 99%?
  3. Profile something. Run a profiler on any program you have and find the hottest function. Was it where you expected?
